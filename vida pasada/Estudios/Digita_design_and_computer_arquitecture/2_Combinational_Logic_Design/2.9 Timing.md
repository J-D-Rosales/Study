On the other hand, propagation and contamination delays are also determined by the *path* a signal takes from input to output. The critical path that is blue, is the path from input A or B to output Y. The path is critical because it limits the speed at which the circuit operates. The short path through the circuit, shown in gray, is from input $D$ to output Y.

The propagation delay of a combinational circuit is the sum of the propagation delays through each element on the critical path.
The contamination delay is the sum of the contamination delays through each element on the short pah.

# 2.9.2 Glitches
It is when a single input transition can cause *multiple* output transitions. These are glitches or hazards.
![[Screenshot from 2025-09-07 18-47-46.png]]
As B transitions from 1 to 0, $n_{2}$ falls before $n_{1}$ (on the critical path) can rise. Until $n_{1}$ rises, the two inputs to the OR gate are 0, and the output $Y$ drops to 0. When $n_{1}$ eventually rises, Y returns to 1. As shown in the timing diagram, Y starts at 1 and ends at 1 but momentarily glitches to 0.
If we want to avoid them, we certainly have to look at the K-map, and see where is taking that transition and add a term, groupings 1's to avoid that. 
In general, a glitch can occur when a change in a single variable crosses the boundary between two prime implicants in a K-map. We can eliminate the glitch by adding redundant implicants to the K-map to cover these boundaries. 
Although we have shown how to eliminate one kind of
glitch, the point of discussing glitches is not to eliminate them but to be
aware that they exist.
